# Proposed exercises

Exercise ideas drawn from [earmaster.md](../earmaster.md) and [ear-training-landscape.md](../ear-training-landscape.md). Just the idea per exercise — sets, levels and mechanics come later. Exercises already written up are marked **done** and point to [outline.md](outline.md).

The file splits in two halves, and the split runs through every domain, not just singing:

**Recognition** — the sound arrives, you name it, write it down, or point at it. The ear does the work alone. Pitch, harmony, rhythm, melodies, describing.

**Reproduction** — you make the sound, by clapping, singing or playing. Needs input and a different kind of feedback, and in every case the answer is judged against something the ear has to recognise first.

Where a domain has both halves, the Recognition section says where its counterpart lives. Only the Reproduction half needs input, which is why it's worth building last.

Source tags: **EM** = EarMaster activity (`EM E8`) or one of its course-level variants (`EM variants`), **L** = landscape exercise (`L E23`). The tags after those are the resources, apps and exam boards that offer the exercise — ABRSM G1–5 means it appears at that grade, not that it's a different kind of exercise.

# Recognition

## 1. Identifying pitch and sound

- **Pitch Comparison** — hear two tones, say which is higher. Needs no theory at all. (L E9 · TH, PE)
- **Absolute pitch naming** — name a note with no reference given. Optional path, evidence is thin. (L E28 · TN, TD, MTN)
- **Interval Identification** — **done** (outline.md)
- **Multi-Note Interval Identification** — **done** (outline.md)
- **Interval in tonal context** — a key is established, then an interval inside it. Context measurably helps. (L E10 · TD, MET)
- **Scale degree in context** — hear a note against a cadence or drone, name its degree. The Functional Ear Trainer method. (L E3 · FET, TD, TH)
- **Scale / mode identification** — hear a scale, name it: major and minor first, then modes, pentatonics, whole-tone, diminished. (L E4 · TD, CET, TG)
- **Consonance or dissonance** — classify a sound as consonant or dissonant, chords inside short progressions included. Beginner-course material. (EM variants)
- **Tuning accuracy** — are these two tones the same, d cents apart; is this note flat, sharp or in tune. (L E37 · TH, Xenharmonium)
- **Notation reading** — name a written note on staff, keyboard or fretboard. Not recognition by ear at all, but it builds the sound–symbol link. (L E17 · MTN, TH, TG)

None of these reproduce anything. The nearest counterpart is single-note pitch matching, in 7.

## 2. Harmony and tonal function

- **Chord quality identification** — **done** (outline.md)
- **Chord inversion identification** — name the inversion: root, 1st, 2nd, 3rd. Bass tone held fixed so only the inversion varies. (EM E5 · L E11)
- **Chord tone or voicing identification** — which chord member is on top, or in the bass: root, 3rd, 5th, 7th. (L E20 · TH, OE)
- **Chord progression identification** — hear 3–8 chords, name the whole progression, or name each chord's function in key. (EM E6 · L E6)
- **Extended and jazz chords** — sevenths onwards into ninths, alterations, ii–V–I and turnarounds. A vocabulary extension of the two above, not a new mechanic. (L E19 · CET, AUR)
- **Cadence identification** — hear a phrase, name how it ended: perfect, imperfect, plagal, interrupted. (L E12 · ABRSM G6–8)
- **Tonic or key finding** — find the tonal centre of a passage played without any reference. (L E18 · TH)
- **Modulation identification** — hear where or to which key the music moves. (L E30 · ABRSM G8)

The counterpart is hearing the bass and then producing it: sing the bass line, or play the bass of a progression back. EarMaster's beginners course puts bass-line singing before chord progressions for exactly that reason.

## 3. Recognising rhythm and metre

Naming or locating a rhythm, without making it.

- **Metre identification** — hear 2–4 bars, say whether it's 2, 3 or 4 time, later the exact signature. (L E15 · ABRSM G1–5)
- **Heard-change detection** — a phrase plays twice; say whether it changed in pitch or in rhythm, or not at all. (L E16 · ABRSM G1–3)
- **Rhythm error detection** — the correct rhythm is shown, a changed one is played, click the notes that differ. (EM E10 · L E16)
- **Beat dictation** — notate a rhythm of plain quarter-note beats, the easiest possible dictation. (EM variants)

The counterparts are in 6: the same material clapped back, tapped out, or read aloud off a staff.

## 4. Melodies: hearing and writing it down

- **Pitch error detection** — a melody is shown, a variant with some notes changed is played, click the wrong ones. (L E8 · AUR, ABRSM G1–3)
- **Melodic dictation, pitch only** — notate the pitches, rhythm given. The classic first dictation. (L E7 · AP, TEO, AUR)
- **Melodic dictation, pitch and rhythm** — both, one to four bars. (EM E12 · L E21)
- **Rhythm dictation** — notate a heard rhythm, on one pitch. (EM E11 · L E23)
- **Tonal threads** — three-note scale-degree fragments inside a key. The exact mechanic isn't documented anywhere. (EM variants)
- **Two-voice dictation** — notate both voices of a two-part texture. (L E22)
- **Bass-line dictation** — notate the bass of a progression, the bass half of harmonic dictation. (L E13 · AP bass part)
- **Harmonic dictation** — soprano and bass plus Roman numerals, AP style. Late, after the earlier dictations are solid. (L E29 · AP, TEO)
- **Transcribe real music** — the same dictation, but the material comes from the score library instead of a generator. (L E38)

Dictation is where pitch, rhythm and harmony all have to be right at once, which is why it sits late in the progression rather than early. Counterpart: the same material played back on an instrument (8). It has no singing counterpart — singing a dictation back is really echo (7), with dictation material as the source.

## 5. Describing what you hear

- **Musical features** — answer questions on dynamics, articulation, texture, character, style, period. (L E35 · ABRSM all grades, Trinity, AP)
- **Instrument or timbre identification** — name the instrument, or which part is louder in a mix. Needs samples. (L E36 · TH)

No reproduction side: you can't clap a style. The closest is the timbre exercise, where the point is telling two sound sources apart.

# Reproduction

You make the sound instead of naming it. Every entry needs input and a per-note or per-tap judgement rather than one right answer, and every entry presupposes that you could already recognise the thing you're being asked to reproduce — which is why this half comes after the recognition half and not alongside it.

## 6. Making rhythm

**From hearing** — you reproduce exactly what you heard, so the difficulty is memory and timing, not analysis.

- **Pulse tapping** — keep time with an excerpt, no notation at all. The earliest rhythm work. (L E26 · ABRSM G1–3)
- **Rhythm clap-back** — hear a rhythm, clap it back. Needs no theory. (EM E9 · L E24)
- **Tap a tune** — keep time with the rhythm of a familiar melody, no pitches. (EM variants)
- **Skipping beats** — tap a steady beat, then leave beats out on purpose. (EM variants)

**From notation** — you play what you read, so reading speed counts too.

- **Rhythm reading** — clap a written rhythm in time. Where rhythm usually starts. (EM E8 · L E25)

Clapping and tapping are the same mechanic with different wording, and the feedback is a mark per tap: correct, early, late, missed. The marking has to be lenient: over-strict rhythm marking is the most common complaint against this kind of exercise, and it's the one thing that makes people quit rather than practise.

Note the contrast with **notation reading** in 1. That one asks you to *name* a written note, so nothing is produced and it stays on the recognition side. This one asks you to *clap* it, so it belongs here. Reading isn't what decides the side — producing is.

## 7. Singing

Split by where the note you're asked to sing comes from. The ear is still the judge in all of them; what changes is what you're given.

**From a name** — the prompt tells you what to sing, so it's the mirror of an identification exercise.

- **Sing a named interval or degree** — hear a reference tone, sing a requested interval above or below it. (EM E3 · L E32)
- **Technical exercises** — sung warm-ups and scale patterns, with a preparatory lesson before each graded one. Course material more than a single exercise. (EM variants · Vocal Trainer, RCM)

**From hearing** — you sing back exactly what you heard, so the difficulty is memory and intonation.

- **Echo or sing-back** — sing a heard phrase back in time. (EM E13 · L E33)
- **Interval sing-back** — sing back an interval you just heard, as opposed to one you were asked for. A different drill from the named version. (EM variants · RCM)
- **Chord sing-back** — sing an arpeggiated triad, or the first three notes of a scale. (EM variants · RCM)
- **Sing the bass line** — sing the bass of a progression. (EM variants)
- **Sing a known tune** — sing a familiar melody, either after hearing it or from a score you've seen. Ties pitch and rhythm together without dictation. (EM variants)

**From notation** — you sing what you read, so reading speed and intonation both count.

- **Sight-singing** — sing written music, ideally graded. A pitch-only free-time variant exists for the lower grades. (EM E14 · L E34)

**Single-note drills** — no phrase, just one pitch held against a target.

- **Pitch matching with live tuning** — sing one note and hold it, with a running cents readout. Where singing usually starts. (L E31 · SC, EL)

Neither document makes a singer identify a *heard sung* sound as its own exercise. The nearest thing is heard-change detection with a voice as the timbre.

## 8. Playing an instrument

Same split as 6 and 7, by where the material comes from.

**From hearing**

- **Play a heard melody back** — reproduce a melody on an instrument, no timing judged. The most requested missing feature in this family. (L E14)

**From notation**

- **Play the score, checked** — play what you read, with pitch and optionally timing judged. The on-screen piano and MIDI-key versions count as instruments here. (L E27)

Everything in 6–8 needs input, and the shared engineering cost is the same for all three sections: a latency-tolerant matcher that turns a stream of performed notes into a per-event judgement against a reference. The only thing that differs is the instrument and how tight the timing tolerance needs to be.

# Session formats

Not exercises themselves, but the shapes a session can take. Worth deciding early because they change what the exercise layer has to expose.

- **Mastery test** — one test per skill across everything, each exam-like. (EM variants)
- **Daily short workout** — five to ten minutes mixing reviews, weak items and the current level. (L 1.9)
- **Mock exam** — a locked sequence of exercises in one board's format, with its own summary. (L 1.9 · AP, ABR)
- **Exam mode** — locked options, limited replays, no hover-to-hear. (L 1.9 · AUR, AP)
- **Hands-free** — loop question, pause, audible answer, no input needed. Recognition only, since nothing is being reproduced. (L 1.9 · SET)
- **Call and response** — question and answer chained with no pause, in a fixed key. Sits between the halves: a recognition prompt, a reproduction answer, no pause between. (EM variants)
